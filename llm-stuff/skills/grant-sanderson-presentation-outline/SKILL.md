# Grant Sanderson Presentation Outline

Design a presentation that lets the audience experience the need for an idea,
build intuition from concrete cases, and participate in the discovery before
receiving the abstraction. In workplace settings, use practical relevance as
the doorway, curiosity as the mechanism, and understanding as the payoff.

## Method references

The primary methodological source is Grant Sanderson's 2023 JPBM
Communications Award Lecture at the Joint Mathematics Meetings,
[“Math's pedagogical curse”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UOuxo6SA8Uc).
Its pedagogical-completion checks include motivating definitions,
rediscoverable reasoning, personal voice, meaningful diagrams, concrete
examples before abstractions, and a motivating question before instruction.
A source transcript is bundled at
`references/maths-pedagogical-curse-transcript.txt`.

The engagement source is Sanderson's 2020 TEDxBerkeley talk,
[“What Makes People Engage With Math”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_L-fp8gDzY).
It distinguishes practical relevance from intrinsic engagement through story,
mystery, emotion, beauty, and surprise, then shows how usefulness and story can
reinforce each other. A cleaned, source-noted transcript is bundled at
`references/what-makes-people-engage-with-math-transcript.txt`.

Use the JMM talk primarily for how understanding should be constructed and the
TEDx talk for why an audience wants to continue. In professional settings,
practical relevance is normally the first engagement engine, but it should not
crowd out the curiosity and conceptual understanding that make the lesson
memorable and transferable.

Read either transcript when checking the fidelity of an interpretation or
extending the methodology. Treat this skill as an adaptation of Sanderson's
ideas beyond mathematics, not as an official framework or a request to imitate
his personal voice. Preserve both clarity and rigor, and do not attribute
additional rules to Sanderson unless a source supports that attribution.

## Establish the presentation brief

Extract these inputs from the request and surrounding context:

- topic and intended learning outcome;
- presentation purpose: decision, approval, training, alignment, persuasion,
  status update, or another explicit job;
- audience, prior knowledge, likely misconceptions, and organizational context;
- practical stakes: the cost, risk, time, customer outcome, opportunity, or
  operational consequence involved;
- desired audience action: what they should decide, approve, understand, or do;
- evidence, confidence, unresolved uncertainty, and required claims;
- total duration, including questions or activities;
- setting: in-person, virtual, or hybrid, plus relevant constraints;
- delivery style: lecture, interactive presentation, workshop, or demonstration;
- supplied sources and required call to action.

Ask only about missing information that would materially alter the outline.
Topic, audience, and duration are normally essential. For workplace requests,
purpose, practical stakes, and desired action are also essential; infer them
only when the context supports a clear conclusion. State consequential
assumptions in the overview. Do not invent business impact, evidence, room,
platform, accessibility, or technology constraints.

## Apply the teaching principles

### Give the audience a reason to care

Connect the subject to a question, experience, or existing interest the audience
already brings. In a workplace presentation, identify the concrete decision,
risk, opportunity, or change that makes the topic matter to this audience now.
Do not manufacture relevance with generic claims about efficiency, innovation,
or ROI; state the specific causal connection and its evidentiary limits.

### Select the engagement engine

Choose practical relevance, intrinsic story, or an interplay of both. Default to
practical relevance in professional settings, then find any narrative tension
inside the work itself: conflicting evidence, an unexpected result, a failed
approach, a consequential tradeoff, or a decision whose answer is not obvious.
Story should carry the substance rather than decorate it.

After relevance has earned attention, look for a concise “curiosity dividend”:
a surprising general pattern, elegant explanation, or broader connection that
helps the audience transfer the lesson beyond the immediate case. Do not force
one when it would distract from the work objective.

### Ignite curiosity early

For teaching, workshops, and exploratory talks, open with a compelling question,
puzzle, surprising observation, or practical problem. Give the audience 30–60
seconds to form an expectation or attempt an answer before supplying definitions
or solutions. In an answer-first briefing, disclose the recommendation and
stakes, then pose the key explanatory question before unpacking the concepts or
evidence. Make the audience feel why deeper understanding is worth their time.

### Move from concrete to abstract

Begin with two or three specific, relatable examples when time permits. Shift at
least one abstraction layer lower than the target idea and populate the
learner's mind with data, cases, quantities, sketches, or familiar objects. Help
the audience notice a shared pattern before naming the general principle. For a
lightning talk, one strong example may be enough; do not mechanically force
three.

### Make visuals do explanatory work

Give every key concept or reasoning step a visual representation when one would
improve understanding. Specify what the audience should see, what changes or
moves, and what relationship the visual reveals. Use the act of designing the
visual to test and deepen the explanation; a strong visual can elicit new
questions for experts while making the entry point clearer for novices. Avoid
vague directions such as “add a graphic” and decorative visuals that compete
with the idea.

### Motivate definitions

Reveal a gap, ambiguity, or recurring pattern before introducing terminology.
Then state the definition plainly and apply it immediately to the motivating
case. Do not lead with a glossary.

### Make the reasoning rediscoverable

Use questions such as “What do you expect next?” and “What obstacle remains?”
so the audience anticipates important steps. For proofs, derivations, and
processes, reconstruct a plausible path of discovery instead of handing down a
finished monument. When useful, use “discovery fiction”: try an appealing but
flawed idea, identify its failure, and repair it step by step, while clearly
labeling what is provisional or false. Allow thinking time. The conclusion
should feel like an insight the audience helped uncover rather than a fact
merely announced to them.

### Use story, personality, and connection

Create a narrative with tension, development, and resolution. Write delivery
notes in a warm, conversational register using “you” and “we,” while preserving
the presenter's own voice rather than impersonating Sanderson. Use analogies,
brief anecdotes, and rhetorical questions only when they clarify the idea.

### Keep rigor and clarity together

Do not treat intuition as a substitute for correctness. State simplifying
assumptions and the limits of hand-wavy explanations honestly. Avoid calling a
step “obvious” or “clear” when a new learner may not see it; reassure the
audience that insight can require exploration. Length is not inherently the
enemy, but every added step should help understanding rather than add verbosity.

## Adapt to the presentation's job

Do not apply delayed revelation mechanically:

- **Decision or executive briefing:** State the recommendation and practical
  stakes early. Do not hide the answer for theatrical effect; use concrete
  examples and guided discovery to make the reasoning inspectable.
- **Training or workshop:** Let participants encounter and attempt the problem
  before instruction, then connect the abstraction back to their work.
- **Alignment, change, or persuasion:** Make the current tension, affected
  stakeholders, tradeoffs, and requested commitment explicit.
- **Status or reporting:** Lead with material changes, implications, exceptions,
  and decisions needed rather than forcing a lesson-shaped mystery.

## Build a feasible structure

Use these proportions as starting points, then adapt them to the purpose and the
answer-first needs of the audience:

- opening — roughly 10%: hook, practical stakes, recommendation when required,
  and motivating problem;
- development — roughly 70%: examples, pattern recognition, and progressive complexity;
- application — roughly 15%: practice, case study, or transfer;
- closing — roughly 5%: memorable synthesis and call to action.

Treat one or two slides per three minutes as a loose planning heuristic, not a
quota. Dense reasoning, demonstrations, discussion, and workshops need more
time per slide. Ensure all slide allocations, activities, transitions, and Q&A
sum to the requested duration.

Adapt the plan to its scale:

- A 5–10 minute lightning talk normally has one narrative arc and no internal
  Q&A break.
- A 15–45 minute presentation can support periodic comprehension checks and,
  when useful, one or more brief question moments.
- A 60–90 minute workshop should prioritize exercises and discussion over slide
  count, with hands-on work approximately every 5–10 minutes when appropriate.
- A virtual or hybrid plan must explain how remote participants can respond and
  how both audiences receive equivalent opportunities to engage.

Never force a poll, Q&A break, or technology-dependent interaction merely to
satisfy a template.

## Output format

Use the following structure.

### Presentation overview

Include workplace-specific fields only when applicable.

- **Working title:**
- **Presentation purpose:** Decision, approval, training, alignment, persuasion,
  status update, or another clearly stated job.
- **Topic and intended outcome:**
- **Audience and assumed prior knowledge:**
- **Practical stakes:** State the audience-specific consequence and causal link.
- **Desired audience action:** State what they should decide, approve,
  understand, or do after the presentation.
- **Engagement engine:** Practical relevance, intrinsic story, or their interplay;
  identify the exact hook and any optional curiosity dividend.
- **Evidence and confidence:** Summarize support, uncertainty, and important limits.
- **Duration, setting, and delivery style:**
- **Total slides:**
- **Total planned time:** Confirm that it equals the requested duration.
- **Narrative arc:** State the motivating tension and eventual resolution. For an
  answer-first briefing, distinguish the disclosed recommendation from the
  explanatory journey that follows.
- **Method:** Concrete-to-abstract and visuals-first, based primarily on
  Sanderson's “Math's pedagogical curse,” with engagement calibrated by “What
  Makes People Engage With Math.”
- **Key interaction points:** Identify slides and interaction types.
- **Flexibility markers:** Identify where to expand and where to compress.
- **Q&A moments:** Include only when they fit the duration and setting.
- **Assumptions:** Include only consequential assumptions.

### Slide-by-slide outline

For every slide, provide:

1. **Slide number and title:** A concise title that advances the narrative.
2. **Content summary:** The essential ideas, examples, or reasoning steps.
3. **Visual elements:** Exact diagram, image, animation, demonstration, or
   visual sequence; explain its teaching purpose. State “none” only when a
   visual would not improve the slide.
4. **Execution notes:** Presenter language, analogy, reveal sequence, and
   guiding questions. Include any intentional pause or audience thinking time.
5. **Tone cue:** For example, curious and inviting, playful tension, aha moment,
   or friendly and reassuring.
6. **Time allocation:** Include interaction time associated with the slide.
7. **Engagement check:** Poll, prediction, think-pair-share, practice task, or a
   brief diagnostic question. If none, explain why uninterrupted flow is better.
8. **Transition:** A specific bridge that creates the need for the next slide.

### Supporting materials

1. **One-page handout:** Specify the exact concepts, diagrams, examples, and
   takeaway action it should contain. It should aid recall rather than duplicate
   every slide.
2. **Resource list:** Give three to five relevant follow-up resources with short
   descriptions. Prefer primary or authoritative sources, verify unfamiliar
   titles and links when tools permit, and never fabricate citations.
3. **Interactive backup plan:** Give a low-technology alternative for failed
   tools or low engagement.
4. **Generation handoff:** Summarize the approved theme, visual assets to create,
   diagrams or animations required, speaker-note needs, target file format, and
   any brand, confidentiality, citation, or accessibility constraints. This is a
   specification for a later deck-generation workflow, not permission to
   generate the deck in this skill.

## Quality check

Before returning the outline, verify that:

- the audience has a compelling reason to care;
- workplace relevance is audience-specific, causal, and evidence-based rather
  than a generic promise of business value;
- practical stakes and the desired audience action appear early and clearly;
- an executive or decision audience is not made to wait for the recommendation;
- the engagement engine fits the context, and story serves rather than decorates
  the substance;
- curiosity and a motivating problem precede formal explanation when the
  presentation's job permits;
- concrete examples precede abstraction whenever time allows;
- every definition has a motivating example and immediate application;
- proofs, derivations, and processes follow a plausible path of rediscovery;
- each important visual has a stated explanatory purpose;
- the explanation feels personal without impersonating Sanderson;
- evidence, uncertainty, simplifying assumptions, and provisional falsehoods
  are labeled honestly;
- guiding questions lead toward genuine reasoning rather than easy theatrics;
- the presentation lowers the entry floor without lowering the intellectual ceiling;
- any curiosity dividend strengthens transfer without obscuring the work objective;
- the narrative has a clear tension, progression, and resolution;
- interactions suit the audience, duration, and setting;
- transitions explain why the next slide follows;
- all time allocations sum exactly to the requested duration;
- expansion and compression points preserve the core story; and
- the response remains an outline and generation specification rather than a
  finished presentation file.
